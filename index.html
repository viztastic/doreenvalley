One

Noise

The developers name the source themselves: the main operational noise is “the inverters and cooling fans required to regulate battery cell temperature.” Those run whenever the batteries work — which, for a plant built to trade on the market, is most of the time.

The valley carries it. We're above it and facing it, across open paddocks with nothing in between. The background sound that would normally mask a hum disappears after dark out here.

Character matters as much as volume. Fans and inverters make a steady, tonal, low-frequency drone. EPA Victoria's guidance says noise “with an unpleasant character is likely to be more disturbing than noise of the same intensity without that character.”

Even where a person complies with the prescribed noise limits, that noise could still be found to be unreasonable — relevant where the impact “is not well represented by the assessment method in EPA publication 1826”, expressly naming low frequency noise.EPA Victoria — Legislative noise framework

So “within applicable limits” isn't the test. Section 166 of the Environment Protection Act 2017 bans unreasonable noise; section 25's general duty runs independently of the limits; regulation 120 makes frequency spectrum a prescribed factor; EPA publication 1996 sets a method for low-frequency noise.

Ask for this

  • Predicted noise at each affected home — day, evening and night — before the decision.
  • Background levels measured in this valley at night.
  • A low-frequency and tonality assessment, not an A-weighted average.
  • Conditions binding noise at the house, verified after construction.
  • Acoustic barriers as a commitment, not a maybe.
Two

The view

The whole site sits in the Green Wedge Zone — zoning that exists to keep this built form out of Melbourne's non-urban landscapes.

And it isn't a paddock with boxes in it. Council's description lists a utility-scale battery, a 220 kV terminal station, underground electrical infrastructure, water storage and firefighting infrastructure, access tracks and operational buildings — plus perimeter security fencing.

Against that, the published mitigation is one sentence: landscaping on the boundaries “to help screen the BESS from view.” That screens someone at the fence, not a house on the ridge looking down. No heights, setbacks, viewpoints or photomontages exist to test it.

Part of the site also carries Environmental Significance Overlay 1. And it's effectively permanent — indicative life to about 2050, with decommissioning left to a permit condition, while the developers concede there's no commercial-scale recycling for these units yet.

Ask for this

  • Photomontages from elevated viewpoints, not road level at the fence.
  • Maximum heights for every structure, terminal station included.
  • Assessment against the Green Wedge Zone's purpose.
  • A funded, bonded decommissioning plan settled before approval.

Two more arguments

Nobody can appeal

The Minister decides under Clause 53.22, so Council is only a commenting party. Council states it plainly: “There are no third-party appeal rights for either Council or the community.”

No VCAT afterwards. The exhibition period is the whole game.

Fire, in a bushfire-prone area

To be accurate: the site is a designated Bushfire Prone Area and, per Council, is not under a Bushfire Management Overlay. The developers concede the substance anyway — “while fires associated with BESS technology are rare, there are risks and hazards that need to be managed.”

Their assessment follows the CFA's renewable energy guidelines. It hasn't been published, and no water supply or separation figures are on the record.
