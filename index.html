One

Noise

The developers name the source themselves: the main operational noise is “the inverters and cooling fans required to regulate battery cell temperature.” Those run whenever the batteries are working — which, for a plant built to trade on the market, is most of the time, including overnight.

Two things make that worse here than it looks on paper.

The valley carries it. We're not behind the noise, we're above it and facing it, across open paddocks with nothing in between. And the background sound that would normally mask it disappears after dark out here. A hum that vanishes in a suburb is obvious on a ridge at 2am.

The character matters as much as the volume. Fans and inverters make a steady, tonal, low-frequency drone. EPA Victoria's own guidance says noise “with an unpleasant character is likely to be more disturbing than noise of the same intensity without that character,” and gives “a high-pitched tonal droning noise” in bedrooms as its example.

“Within applicable limits” isn't good enough

The developers promise their acoustic assessment will meet EPA guidelines and sit “within applicable limits.” Necessary — but not sufficient, and EPA says so itself:

Even where a person complies with the prescribed noise limits, that noise could still be found to be unreasonable — relevant where the impact “is not well represented by the assessment method in EPA publication 1826”, expressly naming low frequency noise.EPA Victoria — Legislative noise framework

There's more to lean on: section 166 of the Environment Protection Act 2017 bans unreasonable noise from non-residential premises, and section 25's general environmental duty runs independently of the limits. Regulation 120 makes frequency spectrum a prescribed factor. EPA publication 1996 sets a method for low-frequency noise specifically.

That's the difference between a plant designed to scrape under a number, and one designed so we can sleep with a window open in February.

Ask for this in your submission

  • Predicted noise at each affected dwelling — day, evening and night — before the permit is decided.
  • Background levels measured in this valley at night, not assumed from a generic rural profile.
  • A low-frequency and tonality assessment (EPA pub. 1996), not an A-weighted average alone.
  • Conditions that bind noise at the house, with verification after construction and a remedy if it fails.
  • Acoustic barriers or enclosures as a commitment, not a maybe.
Two

The view

The whole site sits in the Green Wedge Zone — zoning that exists to keep exactly this kind of built form out of Melbourne's non-urban landscapes.

And it isn't a paddock with some boxes in it. Council's own description lists a utility-scale battery, a 220 kV terminal station, underground electrical infrastructure, water storage and firefighting infrastructure, access tracks and operational buildings. A terminal station is a substation — steel structures, gantries, transformers. Add perimeter security fencing and it reads as industrial from every high point around it.

Against that, the published visual mitigation is one sentence: landscaping on the site boundaries “to help screen the BESS from view.” That screens a person standing at the fence. It does almost nothing for a house on the ridge looking down — the one viewing condition that defines this site. Nothing published addresses that geometry: no heights, no setbacks, no viewpoints, no photomontages.

Part of the site also carries Environmental Significance Overlay 1, protecting habitat and biodiversity, and the land is in an area of Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Sensitivity with a management plan being prepared.

And it's permanent. Indicative life to about 2050, with decommissioning “developed as a condition” later rather than settled now — while the developers concede there's no commercial-scale recycling for these units yet.

Ask for this in your submission

  • Photomontages from elevated viewpoints, not road level at the boundary.
  • Maximum heights for every structure, terminal station and noise wall included.
  • Assessment against the Green Wedge Zone's purpose, not just the energy case.
  • A funded, bonded decommissioning plan settled before approval.
Three & four

The other two arguments

Nobody can appeal

Because the Minister decides under Clause 53.22, Council is only a commenting party. Council states it plainly: “There are no third-party appeal rights for either Council or the community.”

No VCAT afterwards. The exhibition period isn't one step of several — it's the whole game.

Fire, in a bushfire-prone area

To be accurate: the site is a designated Bushfire Prone Area and, per Council, is not under a Bushfire Management Overlay. The developers concede the substance anyway — “while fires associated with BESS technology are rare, there are risks and hazards that need to be managed” — and explain thermal runaway on their own FAQ.

Their fire assessment is to follow the CFA's renewable energy facility guidelines. That's the right document. It hasn't been published, and no water supply, separation or defendable space figures are on the record.
