Not the expected noise at the nearest house, not the distance to the nearest house, and not the acoustic report. Residents are being asked to accept it without those figures.

That may hide the site from someone standing at the boundary. It does nothing for a house on higher ground looking down into the valley.

The land is Green Wedge.

Green Wedge zoning exists to keep industrial development out of Melbourne's rural landscapes. The proposal includes a 220 kV terminal station, transformers and security fencing.

There is no right of appeal.

The Minister for Planning will decide, not the Council. Nillumbik Shire Council has confirmed that neither residents nor the Council can appeal that decision. There is no VCAT hearing afterwards.

We are not against batteries. We are against this location.

Victoria needs battery storage as its coal power stations close. It does not need to be built on the floor of a residential valley, in a bushfire-prone area, with the noise and visual impacts still unmeasured.

Reason one

Noise

The developers name the source themselves. The main operational noise comes from “the inverters and cooling fans required to regulate battery cell temperature.” Those run whenever the batteries are charging or discharging, which for a plant of this kind is most of the time, including overnight.

Two things make that worse at this particular site.

First, the houses are above the plant and facing it, across open paddocks with nothing in between. Second, there is very little background noise here after dark. A hum that would be lost in a suburb is clearly audible on a quiet ridge at two in the morning.

The character of the noise matters as well as the loudness. Fans and inverters produce a steady, low, droning sound. EPA Victoria's own guidance says that noise “with an unpleasant character is likely to be more disturbing than noise of the same intensity without that character.”

The developers say their acoustic assessment will meet EPA guidelines and stay “within applicable limits.” That is necessary, but it is not enough. EPA Victoria says so itself:

Even where a person complies with the prescribed noise limits, that noise could still be found to be unreasonable — relevant where the impact “is not well represented by the assessment method in EPA publication 1826”, expressly naming low frequency noise.EPA Victoria, Legislative noise framework

There is more that a written objection can rely on. Section 166 of the Environment Protection Act 2017 prohibits unreasonable noise from commercial premises. Section 25 imposes a general duty to minimise harm that applies regardless of whether the limits are met. Regulation 120 makes the frequency of the noise a matter that must be considered, and EPA publication 1996 sets out a method for assessing low-frequency noise in particular.

What to ask for

  • The expected noise level at each affected house, for daytime, evening and night, published before the decision is made.
  • Existing noise levels measured in this valley at night, not assumed from a general rural figure.
  • An assessment of the low, droning character of the noise, not just an average reading.
  • Permit conditions that limit noise measured at the house, checked after it is built, with something done about it if it fails.
  • Noise walls or enclosures promised in the permit, not left as a possibility.
Reason two

The view

The whole site is in the Green Wedge Zone. That zoning exists to keep this kind of development out of the rural landscape around Melbourne.

What would be built is not simply a paddock with some boxes in it. Council's description lists a utility-scale battery, a 220 kV terminal station, underground electrical infrastructure, water storage and firefighting infrastructure, access tracks and operational buildings. A terminal station is a substation: steel structures, gantries and transformers. Add security fencing around the perimeter and it looks industrial from every high point nearby.

Against all that, the published mitigation is a single sentence. The project “will include landscaping on site boundaries to help screen the BESS from view.” Planting along a boundary screens a person standing at the fence. It does very little for a house on the ridge looking down, which is the situation most affected residents are in. Nothing published addresses that: no heights, no setbacks, no photographs taken from above.

Part of the site also carries an Environmental Significance Overlay protecting habitat and biodiversity. And the facility is close to permanent. Its expected life runs to about 2050, with removal left to a permit condition to be written later, while the developers acknowledge there is no commercial-scale recycling for these units yet.

Photographs would help a great deal A photograph taken from a real window or deck carries more weight with a planning panel than any drawing. If you can take one looking towards the site, leave your details and we will be in touch about sending it in.

What to ask for

  • Photographs or montages showing the finished plant as seen from houses above the valley, not from the road at the fence.
  • Maximum heights for every structure, including the terminal station and any noise wall.
  • An assessment against the purpose of the Green Wedge Zone, not only the energy case.
  • A funded plan, with money set aside, to remove the plant and restore the land — agreed before approval.

Two further concerns

Nobody can appeal the decision

The application is expected to be decided by the Minister for Planning under a provision called Clause 53.22, which leaves the Council able only to comment. Council puts it plainly: “There are no third-party appeal rights for either Council or the community in relation to the Minister's decision under this process.”

There is no VCAT hearing afterwards. The public exhibition period is not one step among several. It is the only one.

Fire risk

To be accurate about this: the site is in a designated Bushfire Prone Area, and according to Council it is not subject to a Bushfire Management Overlay. The developers acknowledge the risk anyway, writing that “while fires associated with BESS technology are rare, there are risks and hazards that need to be managed.”

Their fire assessment is to follow the CFA's guidelines for renewable energy facilities, which is the right document to use. It has not been published, and no figures for firefighting water supply or separation distances are on the public record.
